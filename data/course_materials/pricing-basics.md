# Pricing a Hair Service: A Simple Starting Point

> Fictional course material for a local portfolio demonstration. The examples are invented and are not financial, tax, or business advice.

## What goes into a service price?

A service price should account for more than the time spent with a client. For a first estimate, list:

- **Time:** the appointment length, plus reasonable preparation and cleanup time.
- **Direct materials:** products and disposable supplies used for the appointment.
- **Business overhead:** the portion of recurring costs such as rent, utilities, booking software, insurance, and payment-processing fees that the business needs to recover.
- **Owner compensation and capacity:** the amount the owner wants the business to generate and the realistic number of appointments available.

## A simple estimate

For a fictional example, suppose a stylist estimates:

- 2 hours of total work for the service, including cleanup.
- $8 of direct materials.
- $12 as an estimated share of overhead.
- $30 per working hour as a planning target for owner compensation.

The simple estimate is:

`(2 hours × $30) + $8 materials + $12 overhead = $80`

This is a planning estimate, not a required market price. It does not account for every tax, fee, discount, or business circumstance. Compare a proposed price with local demand, the actual service scope, and the business's own records before using it.

## Review the estimate

1. Track the actual time and materials for several similar appointments.
2. Compare the estimate with the amount collected, after discounts and payment fees.
3. Check whether the assumed overhead share is reasonable for the number of appointments actually completed.
4. Revisit the estimate when costs, service steps, or demand change.

Do not treat revenue as take-home pay or profit. A qualified bookkeeper, accountant, or other appropriate professional can help with questions about taxes and financial reporting.
